The one-sentence version of this business You find local service businesses whose website is bad or missing, you prove you can fix it before you ask for money, and you charge a price so low that saying yes is easy.

What you are actually selling

You are not selling "a website". Plumbers do not want a website. They want the phone to ring. Your product is: a fast, mobile-friendly page that makes their business look legitimate and makes it obvious how to contact them — handed over as files they own forever, with no monthly fee. That last part is your genuine advantage over every agency charging £49/month forever.

Your honest position as a 16-year-old

You do not need to hide your age and you should not. What you need is proof. A 16-year-old who sends a business a screenshot of a redesigned version of their own homepage is more convincing than a 40-year-old who sends a paragraph about "leveraging digital solutions". Proof beats credentials. This whole manual is built around producing proof cheaply and repeatedly.

Never do this Never claim to be older than you are, never invent a fake company, fake team, fake years of experience, or fake client list. Beyond the ethics: it is the single fastest way to lose a client mid-project and it can void your right to be paid. Age is not your weak point. Lying about it would be.

How to lock in your niche in 20 minutes

  1. Open Google Maps and search mobile car valeting near [your town]. Count how many results appear before the list gets thin.
  2. Open the first 10 listings. Count how many have no "Website" button at all.
  3. Apply the go/no-go test. If 4 or more out of 10 have no website, this niche + area works. If fewer than 4, either widen to a bigger city or move to the next niche on the list.
  4. Repeat for your second-choice niche so you have a backup when the first list runs dry.
  5. Write it down. Cell A1 of your spreadsheet: NICHE: mobile car valeting — AREA: Manchester, Leeds, Liverpool. Do not change it for at least 3 weeks. Niche-hopping is the number one reason beginners get zero results.
On "clients anywhere in the world" You can work with anyone, but you should prospect locally first — the same country and ideally somewhere you can name in the email ("I'm based in [county]"). Local relevance is what makes a cold message not feel like spam, and it's what lets you say "I noticed you're the only detailer in [town] without a site." Expand geographically only after your message is proven: same niche, new city, same script. Do that before you try a new niche.

Method A — Google Maps (do this first, every time)

  1. Open google.com/maps in a desktop browser. Phone is far slower for this; use a laptop.
  2. Search your exact string: mobile car valeting Stockport. Not "car wash" — use the words the businesses use about themselves.
  3. Zoom out slightly so the map covers the whole town, then press the "Search this area" button. This forces Maps to return the full local list instead of just the closest few.
  4. Click the first result, then use the down-arrow key to move through listings one at a time. The left panel updates without a page reload — this is the fastest possible way to scan.
  5. For each listing, look for exactly three things: (1) is there a Website button? (2) how many reviews and what rating? (3) is there a phone number?
  6. Apply the qualification filter (table below). If it passes, it goes in the sheet. If not, skip instantly — do not agonise.
  7. Copy into your sheet: business name, phone, the Maps URL, website URL (or "NONE"), review count, and the area. Takes ~40 seconds per business by hand.
  8. When the town runs dry, change the town, not the niche. Work outwards: your town → nearest city → surrounding towns. A city gives you 60–150 businesses in one niche.
The highest-value 10 seconds in this entire manual A Maps listing with 20+ reviews, 4.5+ stars, and NO website button is your perfect prospect. They are clearly busy and clearly good, they have social proof to put on a site, and they own nothing to compare you against. Flag these in your sheet immediately and contact them the same day. Everything else in this manual is optimisation; this is the actual business.

Method B — Instagram & Facebook (where the no-website businesses live)

A business with 3,000 Instagram followers and no website is the easiest sale in this manual. They already believe in marketing, they already have content, and they already know their Linktree looks amateur.

  1. Instagram search: tap search and enter #carvaletingmanchester, #manchesterdetailing, #mobilevaletinguk. Also search places: tap the Places tab and pick your town.
  2. Open each account and check the bio link. No link, or a Linktree, or a linktr.ee/beacons URL = prospect. A real domain = check it with the scoring system instead.
  3. Check they're active: posted within the last 30 days. Dead accounts waste your follow-ups.
  4. Look for the email button. Instagram Business profiles have an "Email" contact button — that's a public business email, use it. This is often easier than any email-finding tool.
  5. Facebook: search the business type + town, then filter results to Pages. Facebook Pages show a Website field, phone and email in the About tab — often more complete than Instagram.
  6. Local Facebook groups (e.g. "Stockport Recommendations", "[Town] Small Businesses") are dense with tradespeople advertising themselves. Read the group rules before posting anything — most ban unsolicited promotion but allow you to reply when someone asks for a recommendation.
  7. Log the handle in your sheet in the Social column, even when you also have an email. Two channels doubles your chance of a reply.

Method E — Apollo and lead-generation tools

Read this before signing up for anything Apollo, Instantly, Smartlead, Hunter and similar B2B platforms generally require account holders to be 18 or over in their terms of service, and some require a business entity and a card. Check the current terms of any tool yourself before signing up, and if a tool requires 18+, do not create an account by entering a false date of birth. Talk to a parent or guardian — in some cases they can hold the account and you use it with their supervision, which is a normal arrangement for a teenager's business. Full detail in Section 15.

What Apollo actually is: a database of ~250M business contacts with filters (industry, headcount, job title, location) and a built-in email sender. Its sweet spot is companies with employees and job titles — a "Head of Marketing at a 50-person firm". Your prospects are one-person valeting businesses run from a van. Apollo has very poor coverage of exactly the businesses you want. This is the most common expensive mistake beginners make.

Verdict for you: not yet. Google Maps + Instagram will out-perform Apollo for local micro-businesses, at £0. Revisit tools like this only if you move upmarket (5–50 employee firms, £1,000+ sites) later.

The website scoring system

You are scoring how broken the site is. High score = big problem = strong prospect. The whole point is to stop you spending your best hours on businesses that were never going to buy.

Rule 1: No website at all = automatic 100 / Grade A+. Don't score anything else. Put them at the top of your list and contact them first. Selling a first website is roughly three times easier than selling a replacement.

Open their site on your phone first — that's how 70–80% of their customers arrive. The whole scoring pass should take you 90 seconds. Do not analyse deeply; you are triaging, not auditing.

Cold email

Yes, email is the right primary channel — but not on its own, and not the way most people do it.

Is email actually the best method here?

ChannelTypical reply rateUse it for
Email2–8% cold; 10–20% with a mockup attachedYour volume channel. Scales, gets logged, easy to follow up.
Instagram / Facebook DM1–3% cold; 10%+ if warmed firstBusinesses with no website and no listed email. Best for under-30 owners.
Phone call20–40% conversation rateHighest converting thing you can do. Reserve for A+ leads. 10 calls beats 100 emails.

The honest answer: email gets you volume, phone gets you clients. Use email to reach 30 businesses a day, and phone the 3 best ones. If you're nervous about calling, that's normal — start by calling the ones who already replied to your email. That's not a cold call, it's a scheduled chat, and it's much easier.

The five rules every email must follow

  1. Under 90 words. They're reading on a phone between jobs. If it needs scrolling, it gets deleted.
  2. Line 1 must be about them, and must be impossible to have sent to anyone else. This is the whole game.
  3. Name a specific problem, not "your website could be better". "Your site doesn't fit on a phone screen" is a fact they can check in 5 seconds.
  4. One question at the end, easy to answer with one word. Never "let me know if you're interested" — that requires them to write a paragraph.
  5. No attachments in the first email, no links in the first line, no buzzwords. "Synergy", "leverage", "digital transformation", "I hope this email finds you well" — all instant deletes and all spam signals.

Protecting your email reputation

If Gmail decides you're a spammer, your emails silently land in spam forever — and you won't be told. Treat your inbox as an asset.

Do

  • Ramp up slowly. Week 1: 10–15/day. Week 2: 20. Week 3: 30. Never jump from 0 to 100.
  • Use a separate domain for cold outreach if you ever scale — e.g. main site yourname.co.uk, outreach from yournamedesign.co.uk. Damage stays contained.
  • Set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC DNS records on any custom domain. Your domain registrar has a guide; it's 15 minutes and it doubles inbox placement.
  • Send plain text. No HTML templates, no images in the first email, no tracking pixels.
  • Reply to everyone who replies, including "no thanks". Real conversation is a positive reputation signal.
  • Include an easy opt-out line and honour it within 24 hours.
  • Verify addresses before sending. Bounces above ~3% are a serious problem.

Don't

  • Never BCC 50 people on one email. Fastest possible way to get flagged.
  • Never buy an email list. Full of spam traps and dead addresses.
  • Never use spam-trigger phrasing: FREE, GUARANTEED, ACT NOW, £££, all-caps, exclamation marks.
  • Never send from your school email account. It isn't yours, and it may breach your school's acceptable-use policy.
  • Never keep emailing someone who said stop. Beyond the rules, it's just rude — and complaints hurt you the most.
  • Never use a brand-new domain to send on day one. Let it age 2–4 weeks first.
  • Never send 100 identical emails. Identical bodies are a strong spam signal — another reason line 1 matters.

A cold DM from an account the business has never interacted with lands in Instagram's Message Requests folder. The outreach workflow

The complete loop, start to finish. Once it's habit, one prospect takes you about 3 minutes end to end.

  1. FIND — 40 seconds. Google Maps, your niche + town, arrow-key down the results. Skip anything that fails the qualification filter in Section 2. Don't research yet, just collect.
  2. INSPECT — 90 seconds. Open their site on your phone (or Chrome DevTools device mode). Look at it the way a customer would: can you tell what they do, and can you contact them in one tap? Note the two worst problems in plain English — the words you'd use to a friend, not technical terms.
  3. SCORE — 15 seconds. Run the calculator. No site = 100, straight to the top. Under 25 = close the tab and move on, no guilt.
  4. FIND THE CONTACT — 60 seconds. In order: (1) their website's contact page or footer; (2) the Instagram profile's Email button; (3) the Facebook Page About tab; (4) the Google listing; (5) Companies House for the owner's real name; (6) a Google review that names them ("Dave was great"). Stop after 60 seconds — a phone number or a DM is a perfectly good contact route.
  5. ADD TO SPREADSHEET — 30 seconds. Fill every column you have, especially Top 2 Problems. Set Contacted = No.
  6. BATCH. Repeat steps 1–5 until you have 20–30 qualified leads. Do not send anything yet. Research and outreach are different modes of thinking and switching between them halves your speed.
  7. PERSONALISE — 20 seconds each. Open your template. Change: subject line (their name), greeting, line 1 (their reviews/town/photos), and the named problem from column H. Read it once out loud — if it sounds like a robot, cut a sentence.
  8. SEND. One at a time, from Gmail. Log the date. Set the follow-up date. Never BCC.
  9. UPGRADE YOUR TOP 5. Of today's batch, take the 5 highest scorers and build a mockup or record a Loom for them. Send Template C or D instead. This is where your actual clients come from.
  10. FOLLOW UP — 10 minutes daily. Sort the sheet by Follow-up Date, work today's rows, send FU1 / FU2 / FU3, then bump the date. Three follow-ups maximum, then mark Stop.
  11. HANDLE THE RESPONSE — within 1 hour if you possibly can. Speed of reply is a competitive advantage you have over every agency. Use Section 12. Goal of the first reply: get to a phone or video call, or at minimum get them to say a price is fine.
  12. CLOSE. Confirm scope, price and timeline in writing (email is fine). Take a 50% deposit before you start. Send the intake questionnaire (Section 13) the moment the deposit lands. Update Outcome = Won.
  13. BUILD, DELIVER, GET PAID (Sections 13–14). Then immediately: ask for a testimonial and ask "do you know two other [detailers] who'd want the same thing?" Referrals are how this stops being cold outreach.
The three rules that make the loop work (1) Batch by mode — find 30, then score 30, then send 30. Never one at a time. (2) Never skip the follow-up block, even on a bad day; it's 10 minutes and it produces most of your replies. (3) Reply to inbound within the hour. A prospect who emailed you at 4pm has forgotten you by tomorrow.

Getting your first 1–5 clients

You have one good website. That is genuinely enough — if you use it correctly and add one thing to it.

The problem, stated honestly

A dog groomer looking at your portfolio of one unrelated site has to make an imaginative leap: "would this person build something good for me?" Most people won't make that leap. Your job for the first month is to remove it entirely.

Fix 1: build a niche demo (do this before anything else)

  1. Invent a business in your target niche. "Apex Mobile Valeting, Stockport". Make clear on the page that it's a demo.
  2. Build the one-page site you'd sell for £150. Hero with a tap-to-call button, services with prices, photo gallery, three reviews, service area, contact form, footer. Mobile-first. Fast.
  3. Use realistic free stock photos (Unsplash, Pexels) — check each image's licence — and write realistic copy. Not lorem ipsum. Not "Company Name Here".
  4. Deploy it free (Netlify drag-and-drop, GitHub Pages or Cloudflare Pages) so you can send a live link, not a screenshot.
  5. Now you can say the sentence that closes deals: "here's one I built for a detailer — yours would look like this with your photos."
  6. Repeat once more so you have two examples in the same niche. Two same-niche demos beat ten random ones.

Clearly label demos as demos on your own examples page. Never present a demo as a real client's site — that's the kind of small lie that ends a project when it comes out, and it will come out.

Fix 2: the free mockup (your unfair advantage)

Nobody else doing cold outreach at this price point does this, because it takes 25 minutes. That's exactly why it works.

  1. Pick a prospect scoring 70+ with decent photos on their Google listing or Instagram.
  2. Copy your demo template folder.
  3. Swap in their real name, real logo, real colours, 3–4 of their real photos, and 2 of their real Google reviews. 25 minutes, because the structure already exists.
  4. Deploy it to a temporary URL (or just screenshot the top of the page — a screenshot works nearly as well and is faster).
  5. Send Template C. "I redesigned the top of your homepage — no strings, keep it either way."
  6. Expect 3–5 replies from every 10 sent. That's a completely different business from cold email.

One caution: use their photos and logo only for a private mockup shown to them. Don't publish someone's branding on a public site or advert without permission, and take the temporary URL down afterwards if they say no.

Fix 3: the founding-client offer

Say this, exactly, to your first 3 clients:
"I'm building up my portfolio in [car detailing], so I'm doing my first three at £120 instead of £180. All I ask in return is a short written review and permission to show the site as an example. After these three the price goes up."

This is honest, it explains the low price without making you look cheap, it gives them a reason to move now, and it gets you the two things you're actually short of: examples and testimonials.

Fix 4: the warm circle (do this in week 1)

Before any cold outreach, message everyone who already knows you. This is where most first clients genuinely come from.

  • Family members who run anything — a business, a side hustle, a club, a market stall
  • Your parents' friends and colleagues — ask your parents to ask on your behalf, it works
  • Local businesses you or your family already use: the barber, the garage, the gym, the takeaway
  • Your school or college — teachers, the enterprise/careers lead, a sixth-form business club
  • Local sports clubs, scouts, churches, community groups — often desperate for a decent page

Closing the client

Word-for-word answers to the eight things people actually say. Read each one out loud once so it sounds like you, not like a script.

Three principles behind every answer below (1) Answer the question directly and immediately. Dodging a price question destroys trust instantly. (2) End every reply with a question so the conversation can't stall. (3) Try to move it to a phone call. A 10-minute call closes what twelve emails won't.
“How much?”

Say: "A one-page site is £150 — that covers your services, gallery, reviews and a tap-to-call button. If you want separate pages for each service it's £250. Either way it's a one-off, you get the files, and there's nothing monthly. Which sounds closer to what you need?"

Why it works: a straight number, then two options, then a question. Never say "it depends" — that's the answer of someone who's about to make a number up.

“Can I see examples?”

Say: "Yes — here are two I've built for [detailers]: [link] and [link]. Have a look on your phone, that's where it matters. Actually, want me to mock up your homepage in the same style so you can see yours specifically? Takes me half an hour and there's no charge."

Why it works: if your examples are thin, the free mockup instantly replaces the portfolio question with a much better conversation. Turn a weakness into your close.

“I'll think about it.”

Say: "No problem at all. Just so I know I've actually answered everything — is it the price, the timing, or are you not sure what it'd end up looking like?"

Why it works: "I'll think about it" almost always means one specific unspoken objection. This question surfaces it without pressure. Then: whatever they say, answer it and offer one concrete next step ("shall I put the mock-up together and we'll see?"). Add a follow-up date of +7 days in your sheet and actually follow up — a large share of these turn into sales weeks later.

“We already have a website.”

Say: "I saw it — that's actually why I got in touch. On my phone [the text needs zooming in and the number isn't tappable], and most people looking for [a detailer] are on a phone. Not saying you need to replace it today, but would you like me to send a 60-second video showing what I mean? Free, and you can ignore it if you disagree."

Why it works: you never criticise their site as a whole (they may have made it themselves, or paid for it). You name one specific, checkable, unemotional fact and offer proof instead of an argument.

“We're not interested.”

Say: "Understood — thanks for replying, most people don't. I'll leave you to it. If it ever moves up the list, I'm at this address. All the best with [Business Name]."

Why it works: you never argue with a no. Mark them Not interested and stop contacting them. Gracious exits produce a surprising number of enquiries six months later — and if they say "don't contact me again", mark Do not contact and honour it permanently.

“Can you make it cheaper?”

Say: "I can't drop the price for the same work — but I can make it smaller. The £150 one-pager is already my simplest package. If that's still over budget I could do a cut-down version with just the hero, services and contact section for £100, and you can add the gallery later. Would that work better?"

Why it works: you never discount — you reduce scope. Discounting teaches the client your prices are made up and makes every future conversation a negotiation. Trading less work for less money is fair to both of you. A second acceptable move: "I can do £130 if you can pay the full amount up front and get me the photos this week."

“How long will it take?”

Say: "About 7–10 days from when you send me your photos and text — and honestly, that's the part that decides it. If you get me the content on Monday you'll have a preview link by the weekend."

Why it works: it's realistic (never promise 48 hours), and it quietly makes the deadline depend on them sending content. Late content is the single biggest cause of overrunning projects. Always quote longer than you think — delivering early is free goodwill.

“What do I get?”

Say: "You get a complete website as a folder of files — the pages, the styling, and your images, all optimised. You upload it to your own hosting (Hostinger and similar are around £3/month) or I can upload it for you for £30. You own it outright, forever. Nothing to pay me monthly, and if you ever want to move it somewhere else you can, because it's just files. I'll also send you a one-page guide on how to put it live."

Why it works: it turns your limitation (no hosting, no maintenance) into the headline benefit: you own it, no lock-in, no monthly fee. That is a genuine advantage over most agencies and you should say it in every conversation.

Legal & safety considerations

Read this first Nothing here is legal, tax or financial advice, and none of it is guaranteed to be correct for your situation or up to date. Rules differ by country and change over time, and being under 18 changes several of them. Treat this section as a list of things to go and check, not a set of answers. Go through it with a parent or guardian, and for anything involving real money or a dispute, get proper advice from HMRC (or your country's tax authority), Citizens Advice, or a qualified professional.

Contracts

  • Always put the deal in writing, even for a £120 job. The confirmation email in Section 12 is enough for work at this size — scope, price, payment split, revision limit, timeline, what they get at the end. Get a written "yes" back and keep it.
  • Contracts involving under-18s work differently. In England and Wales, contracts with minors are generally not enforceable against the minor in the same way as with an adult, with certain exceptions. What this means in practice varies, and it can cut both ways — including affecting how easily you could enforce payment. Check this with a parent/guardian, and look at Citizens Advice, before you rely on a contract for anything significant.
  • A practical workaround most people use: a parent or guardian contracts with the client and you do the work, or they co-sign. Discuss whether that's right for you. It also gives you an adult to escalate to if a client behaves badly.
  • Say who owns what. Simple and fair: "on final payment, the client owns the website files. The developer may show the work as a portfolio example." One sentence, in the confirmation email.
  • Never promise results. Don't write "you'll rank #1 on Google" or "this will double your enquiries" anywhere. Promise the deliverable, not the outcome.

Payments

  • Almost every payment processor requires the account holder to be 18+ — Stripe, PayPal Business, Wise Business, and most business bank accounts. Check the current terms of any service you're considering. Do not open an account with a false date of birth; if it's discovered your funds can be frozen and the account closed, and it's dishonest.
  • The legitimate options, to discuss with a parent/guardian: (1) a parent/guardian holds the payment account and passes money to you, keeping a clear record of what's yours; (2) direct bank transfer to a young person's account — several UK banks offer accounts from age 11–16, though features and eligibility vary and business accounts usually don't; (3) for very small local jobs, cash with a written receipt.
  • 50% deposit, 50% on approval before file handover. This is the whole of your payment protection at this price point — use it every time, including with people you know.
  • Send a numbered invoice for every job: your name, address (a parent's address is fine — think about whether you want your home address on documents you send to strangers, and discuss it), date, invoice number, description, amount, payment details, due date. Keep every one.
  • Be alert to scams. The classic: someone "accidentally" overpays and asks you to refund the difference — their original payment is then reversed and you're out of pocket. Also: requests to pay by an unusual method, urgency pressure, and anyone wanting your bank login. If it feels off, it is; talk to a parent before acting.

Tax

  • There is no minimum age for paying tax. Being 16 does not exempt you from it.
  • In the UK there is a "trading allowance" which, at the time of writing, lets you earn up to £1,000 of self-employed income in a tax year without needing to register for Self Assessment. Above that you generally need to register as self-employed with HMRC and file a tax return. Check the current threshold and rules on gov.uk yourself — figures change.
  • Keep records from your very first sale: date, client, amount, and any costs (domain, hosting, tools). A tab in your spreadsheet is fine. Doing this from day one costs minutes; reconstructing a year later is miserable.
  • If you're outside the UK, or trading with clients abroad, the rules will differ. Look up your own country's rules and speak to a parent/guardian.
  • Also worth checking: whether income affects any benefits your household receives, and (if you're at school/college) whether there are any rules about paid work during term time.

Refunds and disputes

  • State your refund position in writing before payment: e.g. "the deposit is non-refundable once design work has begun; if you cancel before I start, the deposit is returned in full." Simple and fair.
  • Business-to-business sales generally don't carry the same automatic consumer cancellation rights as selling to a private individual — but a sole trader buying something can sometimes count as a consumer depending on context. Don't guess: if a dispute gets serious, check Citizens Advice.
  • The practical rule that avoids nearly all disputes: a clear written scope, a stated revision limit, a preview before final payment, and replying quickly when something's wrong.
  • If a client refuses to pay the final 50%: you still hold the files. Stay calm and polite, restate the agreement once in writing, and don't threaten. For a £75 balance, chasing it further is usually not worth your time or stress — take the lesson and move on. Talk to a parent before escalating anything.

Cold email rules

  • In the UK, electronic marketing is governed by PECR alongside UK GDPR. A commonly cited distinction is that marketing email to a "corporate subscriber" (a limited company, LLP, or public body) is treated differently from email to an "individual subscriber" — and sole traders and some partnerships are generally treated as individuals, which is more restrictive. Many of your prospects are sole traders. Read the ICO's guidance on direct marketing yourself before you scale up — it's written in plain English and it's free.
  • In the EU the GDPR/ePrivacy position is broadly similar; in the US, CAN-SPAM allows cold B2B email with conditions (accurate headers, a physical address, a working opt-out honoured promptly); in Canada, CASL is stricter and generally requires consent. If you email internationally, the recipient's country's rules matter.
  • Regardless of jurisdiction, always: use your real name and real contact details, never fake the sender or subject line, include a clear way to opt out, act on opt-outs immediately and permanently, and keep a suppression list.
  • Practical safety margin: stay low-volume, highly personalised and genuinely relevant. The businesses you're emailing are ones you can point at a specific problem for. That's outreach, not spam — and it's also just a better way to sell.

Privacy and data protection

  • Your lead spreadsheet is personal data when it contains names, emails or phone numbers of individuals (including sole traders). UK GDPR applies to how you store and use it.
  • Practical steps: keep the sheet private (never "anyone with the link"), collect only what you need for outreach, delete leads you've abandoned, and delete someone's data promptly if they ask you to.
  • If someone asks what data you hold on them, answer honestly and simply, and delete it if they want you to.
  • On client websites: if you add a contact form, the client is collecting data from their visitors. Add a short privacy notice page and mention cookies if you add anything that sets them (analytics, embedded maps or videos can). Tell the client it's their responsibility as the site owner — and don't add analytics or tracking they didn't ask for.
  • Check whether you need to register with the ICO. Some small-scale processing is exempt and there's a self-assessment tool on ico.org.uk. Look it up rather than assuming.

Personal safety

  • Tell a parent or guardian what you're doing, who you're talking to, and how money is moving. Not because you need permission for everything — because someone should know.
  • Don't meet clients alone. Video call, phone call or email is enough for every job in this manual. If an in-person meeting genuinely makes sense, bring an adult, meet somewhere public, and tell someone where you are.
  • Think carefully about your home address on invoices, WHOIS records and your website footer. Ask your parent/guardian about using their address, or use email only.
  • Trust the feeling. If a "client" is pushy, secretive about payment, asks you to do anything you're uneasy about, or wants to move to a private chat app straight away — stop and tell an adult. You're allowed to walk away from any job for any reason.
  • Protect your time. School comes first, and clients who message at 11pm can be answered at 7am. Set the expectation early: "I reply to messages in the evenings."
